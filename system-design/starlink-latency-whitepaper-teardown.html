Starlink Latency: What SpaceX Reports From 48.5ms to 33ms

SpaceX's March 2024 latency whitepaper reports US peak-hour median and p99 changes and lists propagation, ground layout, fronthaul scheduling, and software/buffer delays as drivers. This companion to the core-network outage analysis examines what the document establishes—and what it does not measure.

TL;DR: SpaceX reports that US peak-hour median latency fell from 48.5ms to 33ms (over 30%) and p99 from over 150ms to under 65ms (over 60%, as reported). The paper lists latency-driver categories but gives no measured share for any individual fix. Its stable 20ms median is a goal, not a reported result.

1. The Numbers SpaceX Put on Paper

SpaceX's whitepaper reports median and p99 values, defines peak hours as 6–9 PM local time, and says its anonymized router measurements are averaged every 15 seconds. Those details make the reported figures easier to compare, but they do not replace a per-fix measurement.

One precision note: SpaceX reports the p99 cut as over 60%. From the displayed bounds alone, the reduction is strictly greater than 1 − 65/150 = 17/30 ≈ 56.67%. The 56.67% is rounded; the exact lower bound is 17/30. The whitepaper does not state how it calculated the reported percentage, so keep SpaceX's reported figure separate from the bound implied by the printed values.

The paper reports a larger lower bound for p99 improvement than for the median, which is consistent with improvements to variable delay but does not identify the cause. The PDF itself is undated; the Starlink updates archive dates the post to March 7, 2024. The document cites 2.6 million customers and describes infrastructure work planned for 2024, so treat it as a dated snapshot rather than a current dashboard.

Measurement note: Peak-hour p99 makes tail behavior visible alongside the median. The aggregate change still does not identify which network component contributed how much.

2. The Architecture: Bent Pipe, Extended Pipe, and the Ground That Decides

A Starlink packet uses both a space segment and a ground segment. The space segment links a dish through one or more satellites to a ground station; the ground segment carries traffic over terrestrial links toward an internet connection point or PoP. SpaceX lists propagation, ground layout, fronthaul scheduling and non-physical processing/buffer delays as latency drivers. The whitepaper reports aggregate results, not a measured comparison of the space and ground contributions.

A bent-pipe path carries traffic from a dish to a satellite and ground station, then through terrestrial links toward a Point of Presence (PoP). An extended path can use inter-satellite links before reaching a ground station. SpaceX says laser links can add latency relative to a direct ground path; they can also provide reach or route around congestion. Separately, Mohan et al. (§6.1) report bent-pipe RTTs of 36–48ms, with a median near 40ms across the countries in their RIPE Atlas sample. Their Réunion probe reached a Frankfurt PoP over an inter-satellite path about 60ms faster than the measured terrestrial route. These study results describe sampled paths, not a universal Starlink floor or a decomposition of SpaceX's US peak median.

The July 2025 core-network outage concerns service availability, a different failure from the latency measurements here. The public sources do not establish that the two events shared a root cause or the same path component.

3. Propagation Is One Component, Not an End-to-End Floor

SpaceX estimates physical propagation at 1.8–3.6ms per leg and says it is usually under 10ms for the round trip. Geoff Huston's IEPG slide deck (p. 2) separately gives a 3.7ms minimum propagation delay for a signal to reach a satellite at 550km and return. These are propagation estimates for specified geometries, not a universal end-to-end floor for every terminal/server pair. Ground transport, fronthaul scheduling, processing and queueing add latency.

A useful accounting model is RTT = propagation + ground transport + scheduling + processing/queueing. The terms and paths can vary, and SpaceX publishes no measured allocation of its 48.5ms-to-33ms median change among them. The arithmetic difference is 48.5 − 33 = 15.5ms; it is not evidence that any one category supplied a particular share.

Model propagation separately: state the altitude, slant geometry and route assumed in any speed-of-light calculation. Do not subtract a generic propagation estimate from an end-to-end measurement and label the remainder as ground or queue delay.

4. The Ground and the Air: PoPs and Fronthaul

SpaceX says ground latency depends on network layout and that it was adding six internet connection locations in the US during 2024 while optimizing gateway placement and user allocation. That is the company's stated plan, not a measured estimate of how much the reported median changed because of PoPs. Mohan et al. (§4) separately report that Starlink's local Philippines PoP coincided with a 90% reduction in latency to in-country M-Lab servers after May 2023; their study also describes a Réunion-to-Frankfurt route. These are site-specific observations, not a per-fix decomposition of the SpaceX whitepaper.

SpaceX identifies fronthaul scheduling—the radio links between a satellite and its users—as an inherent but optimizable latency driver. Tanveer et al. report 15-second allocation marks at four tested terminals in the US and Europe. Mohan et al. report link changes at two European sites; in one selected single-satellite window, a satellite-to-satellite switch could not explain the change. Casparsen et al. attribute similar spikes to handovers in their separate Aalborg traces. The populations and test setups differ; the companion scheduler analysis keeps those findings separate.

The design signal: gateways, PoPs and scheduling each affect a different part of the path. Measure those segments separately before assigning a latency reduction to one of them.

5. The Dumb Stuff: Buffers, Queues, and the WiFi in Your Hallway

SpaceX calls unneeded processing delays, unoptimized buffers and packet drops that force retries “dumb stuff.” The company reports right-sizing buffers, improving queueing algorithms on gateway links and adding fq_codel active queue management to its WiFi router. It does not report a separate latency gain for each action, so the p99 change cannot be assigned to these fixes from this document alone.

Mohan et al. (§4) report RTT inflation of roughly 2–4× during active downloads, reaching 400–500ms in their M-Lab speed-test analysis. The authors discuss bufferbloat as a possible explanation; those measurements are a separate corpus and do not quantify SpaceX's later buffer or fq_codel changes. Queue management can help under contention, but its effect depends on the path, device and workload.

SpaceX also reports that, since the beginning of 2024, teams had deployed and tested 193 satellite software builds, 75 gateway builds, 222 Starlink builds and 57 WiFi builds. Those four category counts sum to 547; they are not a count of unique cross-fleet releases or evidence that the builds caused the latency change. A fast release cadence and staged rollout discipline are separate engineering questions; the Cloudflare edge teardown documents why rollout controls matter.

7. What to Steal: The Price of a Millisecond

SpaceX gives a web-page example: its testing found that increasing bandwidth beyond around 10 Mbps did not increase page-load time, while reducing latency did. That statement is the company's example, not a result for every workload. The table below marks which application claims the whitepaper did and did not test.

WorkloadDesign considerationWhat the whitepaper establishes
Cloud gaming at 60 fpsOne frame interval is 1,000/60 ≈ 16.7ms; RTT is not the same as one-way input-to-display time.The whitepaper does not test gaming or establish playability from the reported p99 change.
Video callsCompare conversational delay and loss against the application's own targets.No call-quality test appears in the latency whitepaper.
Web page loadsSequential network round trips can add to load time; caching and request graphs matter.SpaceX gives a general example above around 10 Mbps; it does not quantify a site-specific page-load improvement.
Realtime trading / opsMeasure tail latency and jitter against the workload's deadline.No trading workload or operational SLA is tested in the whitepaper.
  1. Publish p99 at peak. State the percentile, time window and sample base, then separate vendor-reported endpoints from derived bounds. Adopt the same discipline for your own SLOs and burn budget with the error-budget calculator.
  2. Measure buffers under contention. Queueing can add delay without packet loss. Audit queue depth and tail latency before buying capacity.
  3. Evaluate fair queueing on paths you control. SpaceX reports adding fq_codel to its router; test active queue management against your own workload and equipment.
  4. Measure placement changes. SpaceX said it was adding six US PoPs and optimizing allocation; its whitepaper does not isolate the latency gain from those actions.
  5. Stage path-selection and software changes. SpaceX reports microsecond subsystem monitoring and lower-latency path selection; its build counts do not establish how widely each build was deployed or which change caused the aggregate improvement.
The useful distinction: SpaceX reports the improvement and lists its driver categories. The public paper does not split the measured change among those drivers.

Appendix A. Reference Posture: Trusting a Vendor Latency Number

The checklist I would run before quoting any provider's millisecond:

  1. Percentile, window and sample base. State p50 or p99, peak or all-day, and whether the denominator is router averages, active probes or a lab test.
  2. Keep tail behavior separate. A larger p99 reduction can be consistent with queue changes, but it does not identify a unique cause. Compare it with other evidence before assigning a mechanism.
  3. Use the publication record for dates. The PDF is undated; Starlink's archive lists it on March 7, 2024. Do not infer its publication date from the customer count or deployment plans in the text.
  4. Require per-fix attribution or state that it is absent. SpaceX publishes aggregate results and driver categories, not a measured share for each fix.
  5. Keep independent studies separate. Mohan et al.'s multi-country dataset, Tanveer et al.'s four-terminal scheduler study, and Casparsen et al.'s Aalborg traces use different populations and methods; they do not jointly establish one cause for every latency change.
  6. Link to current measurements with their limits. The whitepaper points readers to Starlink's map; that does not make its dated measurements current telemetry.

Pair this review with the web performance course and re-run it whenever a vendor refreshes its numbers. Latency claims rot faster than any other metric because the network underneath never sits still.

Appendix B. Transport Measurements Under Periodic Link Changes

This transport discussion is separate from SpaceX's whitepaper. Mohan et al. (§3.1) say the M-Lab NDT7 component uses TCP BBR; that 19.2-million-test corpus is not a CUBIC comparison. Geoff Huston's IEPG slides (pp. 16–20) show CUBIC, QUIC/CUBIC and BBR plots under the deck's measurement conditions and argue that loss-tolerant control can help. The slides are one set of measurements, not a universal ranking of congestion controls across Starlink paths.

These transport plots answer a different question from the latency whitepaper. They do not establish which congestion-control algorithm is best for every Starlink path, and they do not split the whitepaper's reported median improvement among network components.

For a workload you control, compare transport settings on the same path and in the same test window. Record packet loss, useful throughput and completion-time percentiles; keep the protocol version, test load and connection endpoints with the result. These sources do not establish one universally superior congestion-control algorithm for all Starlink traffic.

Test it yourself: compare CUBIC and BBR on a path you control, with the same endpoints and workload. Treat the result as specific to that path and test window.

Appendix C. The Drill: Measure Your Own Sky

A site-specific study needs its own measurement protocol. Keep the raw sample cadence, clock synchronization, endpoint locations, terminal firmware and test load with the results.

Instrument. Use a wired host to separate WiFi from the satellite path. Record RTT, loss and useful throughput to a near and a farther destination. If attempting to observe sub-second changes, use a high-rate probe and state its rate: Mohan et al.'s controlled tests sampled RTT every 3ms and throughput every 100ms. SpaceX's whitepaper instead reports 15-second average router measurements; that cadence is not sufficient to reveal the detailed shape of a sub-second boundary event.

Capture peak. SpaceX defines peak hours as 6–9 PM local time. Record p50 and p99 for a clearly defined window, with the sample count and missed probes. If testing candidate 15-second boundaries, treat them as a hypothesis from other studies, not as an established schedule for every terminal.

Compare paths without over-decomposing. A near and far target can reveal a difference in end-to-end RTT, but does not by itself isolate fronthaul, PoP routing or satellite propagation. Report what was directly measured and keep the remaining components unresolved unless the path instrumentation identifies them.

Separate WiFi load: compare wired and WiFi measurements under the same background transfer. The difference estimates the effect of that household setup; it does not isolate fq_codel or prove a universal bufferbloat reduction.

8. The Verdict

SpaceX's whitepaper establishes the reported aggregate improvement and names four driver categories. It does not measure the contribution of each fix, show that the propagation estimate matches the before/after median sample, or establish that the 20ms goal was reached. Independent studies add separate evidence: Mohan et al. analyze multi-country speed tests and controlled European terminals; Tanveer et al. infer global allocation patterns from four terminals; Casparsen et al. attribute boundary spikes to handovers in a different testbed. Their scopes and explanations should remain distinct.

15.5ms is the difference between two reported medians. The per-fix shares remain unpublished.

What to watch next: whether SpaceX publishes current-era latency measurements, absolute non-US baselines, or a measured split by driver. For your own network, separate propagation estimates from observed end-to-end latency and test each controllable path segment directly.
