Starlink's 15-Second Rhythm: Evidence for a Global Scheduler

Independent teams have reported recurring 15-second Starlink allocation or link-reconfiguration intervals. Tanveer et al. identify satellite allocations at fixed minute marks from four US and European terminals; Mohan et al. measure aligned link changes at two European sites. The studies support a global-scheduler hypothesis, but they do not show every dish changing at every mark or settle which resources change.

TL;DR: Tanveer et al. report satellite allocations in 15-second slots and infer a global controller from four terminals. Mohan et al. observe latency and throughput changes aligned across Munich and Edinburgh; in one selected window with only one satellite candidate, a satellite-to-satellite switch cannot explain the change. That result does not establish the cause of every boundary event. A 2026 Aalborg study attributes similar latency spikes to handovers in its own traces, so the causal explanation remains contested.

1. The Measurements: Four Terminals and Two Controlled Dishes

Mohan et al. (ACM Web Conference 2024, §§3.3 and 6.2) measured Starlink terminals in Munich and Edinburgh. They shielded the Edinburgh terminal from the south, restricting it to the less-dense 70° and 97.6° orbital shells and reducing the candidate set to 13% of its unshielded size. Using external satellite tracking, they identified several connectivity windows in which only one satellite could serve the terminal. Their instruments sampled RTT every 3 ms and throughput every 100 ms.

The two sites showed latency changes at aligned 15-second intervals; throughput also fell at interval boundaries. In the selected Edinburgh window with one candidate satellite, the changes continued. A switch from one satellite to another cannot explain that window. Mohan et al. infer periodic network reconfiguration and suggest that the same satellite may remain assigned while frequency or routing resources change. They do not identify which resource changed.

Tanveer et al. (2023, §§3–6) measured four terminals—one in Western Europe and three in the United States—and reported RTT changes at :12, :27, :42 and :57 past the minute at all four measured locations. They also used obstruction maps and satellite position data to infer which satellite served each terminal in a slot. The authors interpret these observations as evidence of a global controller; the measurements do not cover every terminal or every region.

2. The Architecture: Two Inferred Control Layers

Tanveer et al. (§§5–6) describe two inferred control layers: a global controller that assigns satellites to terminals and a local on-satellite controller that schedules user flows. Their allocation data show patterns in the selected satellites. Across their four terminals, selected satellites had a median elevation 22.9° higher than available but unselected satellites. When both sunlit and dark satellites were available, the selected satellite was sunlit in 72.3% of the measured slots. The study's model achieved 65% top-five accuracy for satellite characteristics, compared with a 22% baseline. That is a model of observed selections, not disclosure of SpaceX's algorithm or proof of its objective weights.

The paper also reports parallel latency bands within some 15-second intervals and treats them as evidence of a somewhat round-robin on-satellite scheduler, while noting that more work is needed to identify its exact behavior. It explicitly notes that the model lacks private inputs such as terminal density and satellite load. Those limitations matter: the public data support hypotheses about selection patterns, not a complete account of the live scheduler.

3. What the Single-Satellite Window Does—and Does Not—Show

The single-candidate result has a narrow but useful implication: satellite-to-satellite switching cannot account for the changes in that selected window. It does not establish that the same mechanism explains all periodic changes. Casparsen et al. (2026, §§2–4) analyze 500 Hz end-to-end traces from an Aalborg testbed and describe boundary latency spikes in their traces as handover-induced. Their measurements and interpretation do not resolve the difference with Mohan et al.'s controlled window.

Casparsen et al. report an average peak about 74 ms above the mean in the plotted uplink profile, with boundary windows of roughly 140 ms at the start and 75 ms at the end of each 15-second period. Those are measurements from their testbed, not a universal Starlink service guarantee. Tanveer et al.'s exact minute marks and the cross-site alignment support a periodic, coordinated pattern at their measured terminals; neither study establishes that every dish is re-examined at every mark.

SpaceX has not published the scheduling algorithm. The public evidence supports periodic satellite allocations and link changes at tested sites, but leaves open the changed resource, the controller's full scope, and whether the same cause explains every boundary event. Monitoring should record boundary timing alongside terminal status and traffic data, rather than automatically classifying a boundary-aligned change as either a handover or a load-balancing action.

4. Application Effects: Measure the Boundary, Not Just the Average

Mohan et al. (§5) tested Zoom and Luna cloud gaming over Starlink. Their 150-minute Luna evaluation showed occasional frame-rate drops below 20 FPS that coincided with reconfiguration intervals; their paper also reports boundary-aligned changes in RTT and throughput. Those are results from the authors' test setup, not a guarantee that every application or Starlink path behaves the same way.

The studies do not establish that one congestion-control algorithm is universally better for these events. For a real-time workload, record latency, loss, useful throughput and application deadlines across multiple intervals, and compare transport settings on the same path. The latency teardown discusses other measurement limits in Starlink performance data.

For gaming, calls, teleoperation or other deadline-sensitive services, compare the measured boundary behavior with the application's actual deadline. Use queue models such as the backlog drain calculator only when measured arrival and service rates support the inputs; a periodic link change is not automatically a queue micro-burst.

6. Practical Guidance: Correlate Before You Classify

  1. Keep packet-level timestamps. Aggregate summaries can hide brief boundary effects. Preserve the time zone, measurement interval and clock-synchronization method.
  2. Plot the reported marks as hypotheses. Compare measurements against :12/:27/:42/:57, then check whether the pattern appears in your own site and observation period.
  3. Correlate with terminal status. Record available connection or obstruction data alongside latency and throughput. Timing alone does not identify a satellite or a changed resource.
  4. Keep periodic changes and incidents distinct, but don't assume the cause. A boundary-aligned deviation may be part of a recurring pattern; verify it against the system's status data before suppressing or paging on it.
  5. Test the application deadline. Measure loss, useful throughput, recovery time and application outcomes across several intervals under the workload you actually run.

7. Estimating Exposure Without Inventing a Cost

The Aalborg study's plotted uplink profile marks about 140 ms at the start and 75 ms at the end of each 15-second cycle. That is a 215 ms measurement window per cycle. If the same pattern persisted for an hour, the marked windows would total 240 × 0.215 seconds = 51.6 seconds per hour, or 51.6 ÷ 3,600 = 1.43% of elapsed time. This is arithmetic on that study's marked windows, not a claim that every Starlink terminal loses service for 51.6 seconds each hour.

The study reports latency increases, not the number of affected customers, application failures, or a monetary loss. A workload-specific model would need measured affected sessions, degraded seconds per session, and a defensible value per degraded second. Without those inputs, a dollar estimate would create precision the sources do not provide.

Appendix A. A Small Measurement Plan

For a site-specific check, use a wired host and record timestamped RTT, loss and throughput over a sustained period. Keep clock synchronization, terminal firmware, location, weather and test load with the data. Plot the reported minute marks as candidate boundaries, then compare them with terminal status and available orbital data. A recurring statistical difference can identify a pattern in that dataset; it does not by itself identify the cause or establish a global rule.

Do not use a single p-value as proof of a scheduler. Report the number of intervals, sample rate, missing data, test conditions and uncertainty, and distinguish a measured correlation from an inferred mechanism.

8. The Verdict

Measurements from four US and European terminals identify 15-second satellite-allocation patterns, while controlled tests at two European sites find aligned link changes and rule out satellite switching in one selected single-candidate window. These results support a global-scheduler hypothesis but do not establish that every dish changes at every boundary or identify one cause for all observed spikes. A separate 2026 study attributes similar latency spikes to handovers in its traces. The scheduler's implementation, the role of handovers outside the controlled window and the extent of the pattern remain unknown.

The useful operational conclusion is narrower: measure at enough resolution to see recurring changes, correlate them with terminal status, and keep the mechanism labeled as uncertain until the data distinguish it.

Sources and Method

Claims in this article distinguish the studies' measurements from their interpretations. Tanveer et al. report four terminal locations and infer global allocation control; Mohan et al. report two controlled European sites and a selected single-candidate window; Casparsen et al. report a separate Aalborg measurement campaign and interpret its latency spikes as handover-induced.
