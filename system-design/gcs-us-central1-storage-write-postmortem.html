GCS us-central1 Writes: 12h30, Then a 5h41 Recurrence

Google's October 14 final report records two Cloud Storage write-impact windows: 12 hours 30 minutes in us-central1 on October 8, followed by a 5-hour-41-minute recurrence in us-central1-b on October 9. The reported active durations total 18 hours 11 minutes, not one continuous outage.

TL;DR: A traffic-sorting configuration bypassed standard capacity-aware routing and concentrated writes on backend nodes with little serving capacity. An incomplete rollback of an upload-planner feature caused the next day's recurrence. The report gives two windows and does not publish node counts, traffic rates, affected-customer counts or financial losses.

By Mukul Kumar Mishra · Evidence-led system design postmortem · Updated October 17, 2026

1. Two write-impact windows, not one continuous outage

Google's final report, posted October 14, separates the incident into two periods, all times US/Pacific. The first ran October 8, 04:00–16:30, in us-central1: 12 hours 30 minutes. The recurrence ran October 9, 04:05–09:46, in us-central1-b: 5 hours 41 minutes. Google reports an active total of 18 hours 11 minutes.

The listed windows are separated by 11 hours 35 minutes (October 8, 16:30 to October 9, 04:05). From the first start at 04:00 on October 8 to the final end at 09:46 on October 9 is 29 hours 46 minutes elapsed. That elapsed span includes the interval between the two reported windows; it is not the report's active-duration total.

Customers primarily saw HTTP 503 UNAVAILABLE on WriteObject and client-side timeouts; successful writes had elevated latency. Google's impact section says other regions and other Google Cloud services were unaffected by this event.

2. A routing configuration bypassed capacity-aware placement

In the final report's root-cause account, an update to the Cloud Storage load-balancing layer was meant to address localized capacity constraints. The update bypassed standard capacity-aware routing. A small fraction of storage backends then received an overwhelming share of writes even though those nodes had little available serving capacity. The affected object-ingestion services timed out, so writes routed to them became slow or failed.

The report describes a separate underlying-database issue that added metadata-processing errors during part of the first incident window. It does not present that database fault as the cause of the next-day recurrence; it identifies the recurrence separately as an incomplete rollback of an upload-planner feature.

3. The capacity constraint is simple; the public numbers are not there

For each backend, incoming write work must fit within its available serving capacity: offered load ≤ available capacity. The final report says the routing configuration could bypass that capacity-aware check and gives the resulting imbalance in qualitative terms. It publishes no backend counts, write rates, capacity values or per-node measurements, so the report supports the failure mechanism but not a numerical reconstruction of the load imbalance.

The important boundary is that an optimizer can only protect capacity if its routing decision still respects the capacity limit. If a configuration path can bypass that limit, a region may have unused capacity and still overload the small set of nodes receiving the misrouted work.

4. Initial mitigation gave temporary relief; an incomplete rollback allowed recurrence

Google first tried to allocate emergency capacity, but regional capacity limits blocked that step. Engineers then drained traffic from the most overloaded nodes, disabled the traffic-sorting optimization and rolled back a recent metadata-database update. Those actions provided temporary relief.

Google attributes the October 9 recurrence to an incomplete rollback of an upload-planner feature that broke load balancing and overloaded a few smaller storage backends. The report says the incident was permanently resolved when engineers fully reverted the problematic load-balancing configuration, restoring the previous capacity-aware routing behavior. Drained nodes returned to service after traffic and resource use stabilized.

The distinction is operationally important: disabling one optimization and partially rolling back a feature did not remove every changed path. The final resolution required a full configuration revert.

5. Guardrails supported by the final report

Google lists three prevention efforts: validate load-balancing changes in smaller, alternate regions before larger high-utilization regions; enforce capacity constraints so routing cannot send work to clusters without enough serving resources; and improve internal data movement in highly utilized storage cells.

For an infrastructure review, make those goals testable: reject a routing configuration that bypasses capacity checks; roll the change through a small region before a high-utilization one; track every component changed by an upload-planner release; and rehearse a complete revert, including traffic drain and restoration. These are Buildopsy recommendations based on the published failure sequence, not claims that Google had no other safeguards.

6. Verdict: the final report explains both windows, but not their scale

Google's final report establishes a capacity-aware-routing bypass as the first write failure's mechanism, a separate metadata issue during part of that window, and an incomplete upload-planner rollback as the cause of the next day's recurrence. It records 12h30 and 5h41 of impact, totaling 18h11 across the two windows.

The report does not disclose node counts, incoming write rates, per-node capacity, affected customer counts or customer losses. The public record therefore supports the configuration failure and recovery sequence, but not a quantitative load model or financial estimate.

Sources and Method

I checked Google's final incident report, posted October 14, 2026 at 00:15 PDT: “Incident First Occurrence,” “Incident Recurrence,” “Root Cause,” “Remediation and Prevention” and “Description of Impact.” The duration arithmetic is reproducible: 12h30 + 5h41 = 18h11 reported active time. Buildopsy calculates 29h46 from October 8 at 04:00 to October 9 at 09:46; subtracting 18h11 leaves the 11h35 interval between the reported windows. Those two elapsed-time figures are calculations, not additional Google-reported impact durations. This update uses the final report rather than the October 9 preliminary report.
