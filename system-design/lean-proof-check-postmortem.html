Lean Proof Checking: What OpenAI's Navier–Stokes Repository Reports

OpenAI's public repository reports zero sorry uses in its main results, lists their axiom dependencies, and pins Lean 4.34.0-rc2. Its README also documents build and Comparator instructions. Those are project disclosures; an October 6 preprint separately reports paper-to-Lean mismatches without judging whether the natural-language proof is correct.

TL;DR: Lean checks that proof terms establish the encoded propositions under the recorded environment. The OpenAI manifest reports zero sorry, lists three standard axioms for its main results, and marks review as self-assessed. That does not establish that every formal statement matches the paper.

By Rook research desk · AI claim audit · Updated October 23, 2026

Public record: OpenAI published its writeup and Lean repository on September 8, 2026. The repository includes a self-reported manifest and Comparator instructions. An October 6 preprint reports two paper-to-Lean discrepancies; it does not conclude that the written proof is false.

1. What OpenAI Published

OpenAI's September 8 announcement says its internal system produced a finite-time blow-up result for Navier–Stokes statements C and D in the Clay formulation, and links both the paper and a Lean repository. The company reports that its agents reached the Navier–Stokes result about 88 hours after launch and that Lean formalization and verification took another 17 hours. It also reports 2.7 million messages and approximately 130 billion output tokens for this problem. These are OpenAI-reported process figures.

The repository makes its formal files public. A later, October 6 preprint by Bastounis, Circelli and Hansen compares selected paper estimates with cited Lean declarations and reports mismatches. The authors explicitly do not assess whether the natural-language proof is correct. The preprint is a separate analysis published nearly a month after OpenAI's announcement, not a same-week competing proof.

The distinction that matters: a Lean build checks the formal statements and proof terms in its environment. It does not automatically show that those statements preserve every claim or argument in the paper.

2. What the Kernel Guarantees

Lean's trusted kernel checks proof terms against their encoded types. Tactics, automation and language-model-generated search can propose terms, but those terms must still pass the kernel. Assuming the kernel and its declared axioms are sound, an invalid term cannot pass that check.

The kernel establishes that the term proves the proposition encoded in the environment. It does not establish that the proposition matches the intended theorem, that the paper's arguments were faithfully translated, or that the build used the expected toolchain.

3. What It Does Not

sorry can fill a proof obligation. Lean's reference explains that the tactic relies on sorryAx, which can inhabit any type, and says it should not occur in finished proofs. OpenAI's repository manifest reports sorry_count: 0 and sorry_in_definitions: 0 for the project and its listed main results. That is a repository declaration, not an independent check of every source file.

Axiom dependencies can be inspected. Lean's #print axioms command lists the axioms used by a declaration, directly or transitively. OpenAI's manifest lists propext, Classical.choice and Quot.sound for its main results; Lean documents these as standard axioms. The public metadata does not report a custom axiom for those results.

A different theorem can check fine. The kernel verifies terms against encoded statements; it has no access to the author's intent. A definition or theorem statement that differs from the paper can still have a valid proof term. Correspondence is a separate mathematical question.

Reproducibility depends on the recorded environment. OpenAI's README identifies Lean 4.34.0-rc2 and documents Lake and Mathlib build steps, and separate Comparator commands. A pinned toolchain makes a rerun more reproducible; it does not establish that the formal statement faithfully represents the paper's intended statement.

A useful audit reads the proposition, its dependencies and the toolchain together, then compares the formal statement with the source paper.

4. The Checklist

Print the axioms for the declarations that matter, then compare the output with the project manifest. OpenAI's manifest lists three standard axioms for its main results. Any difference between source and manifest is a question to investigate, not automatic evidence of a flaw.

Check sorry use in the source and final declarations. OpenAI's manifest reports zero for the project and its main results; that is a repository declaration, not an independent source audit.

Preserve the recorded environment. OpenAI's repository pins Lean 4.34.0-rc2 and gives Mathlib/Lake build instructions. Record the exact commit and build flags when reproducing it.

The README also documents Comparator challenges for Navier–Stokes and Euler. The manifest marks review as self-assessed; its build instructions provide a route for independent checking, not a receipt that such a check has already happened.

Read the statement like opposing counsel. The kernel checks terms against the encoded proposition; it cannot decide whether that proposition preserves the paper's intended theorem. Compare definitions and assumptions before treating a successful build as evidence about the written proof.

5. Applied to the Public Repository and Preprint

The repository's formalization.yaml claims full formalization of its listed main results, zero sorry uses, and the three standard axiom dependencies. It marks the review status as self-assessed. The README pins Lean 4.34.0-rc2 and documents Lake and Comparator commands. These files disclose a version, axioms and a route for independent checking; the repository does not provide an independent review receipt.

The October 6 preprint reports two paper-to-Lean differences: an m+4 versus m+5 derivative requirement and a pressure-flux estimate whose formal version also depends on another quantity. The authors say these differences limit what the cited Lean estimates establish about correspondence; they do not conclude that the written proof is false. The mathematical effect remains for expert review.

6. The Verdict

OpenAI has published a paper, a Lean repository, a project manifest and instructions for external Comparator checks. The manifest reports zero sorry use, lists the standard axioms for the main results, and labels its review as self-assessed. The October 6 preprint reports two mismatches in cited estimates; its authors do not judge the natural-language proof itself.

Kernel checking and paper-to-code correspondence answer different questions. The public files document a pinned route to reproduce the first. Whether the formal statements faithfully capture every relevant estimate in the paper—and what the reported mismatches mean for the full argument—remains open.

OpenAI reports a Lean formalization. The paper-to-code correspondence still needs review.
