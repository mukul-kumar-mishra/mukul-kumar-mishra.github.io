OpenCode Console Breach: The Missing Link in the Distillation Claim

On October 17, 2026, OpenCode said a breach related to a “large-scale distillation attack” had impacted 1.4% of OpenCode Console users. The company described a possible exposure of external-provider keys; users then reported unexpected API charges. Public posts from Anthropic, Google, and OpenAI document containment or credit actions. None of those posts establishes who used an exposed key, or that its use produced training data.

TL;DR: The public record supports a reported Console breach, a company-stated 1.4% share, a customer notice warning that provider keys may have been exposed, several user-reported charges, and provider actions. It does not disclose the number behind 1.4%, a confirmed intrusion start or entry method, audited losses, or evidence connecting key use to model training. The local OpenCode app is a separate surface; co-founder Dax Raad said its locally configured keys “never come to us.”

By Rook · AI incident disclosure audit · Published October 24, 2026 · Research cutoff October 24, 2026

Verdict: The breach and response are publicly documented. “Distillation” is OpenCode’s characterization; the public evidence reviewed here does not establish the attacker’s identity, the use made of any exposed key, or a connection to model training.

1. The denominator is Console users, not every OpenCode install

OpenCode’s October 17 post said the incident affected 1.4% of “OpenCode Console users,” and that the notified subset had external providers linked. It did not publish the numerator or the total Console-user count, or say whether “impacted” meant users notified, accounts with a linked key at risk, or keys confirmed exposed. The public number therefore gives a share, not an absolute count of people or credentials.

That boundary matters. The hosted Console is not synonymous with the open-source CLI and desktop app. In a reply on October 17, co-founder Dax Raad said the incident concerned “the OpenCode Console, not OpenCode itself,” and that keys configured locally “never come to us.” That is an attributed company-side distinction, not evidence about the unknown entry path into the Console system.

The customer notice is available here only through reporting. Infosecurity Magazine’s October 19 report quotes the email as identifying unauthorized access to “a legacy system that we were in the process of decommissioning.” The notice, as transcribed by The Hacker News and shown in a BleepingComputer screenshot, reportedly warned that provider keys saved in workspaces “may have been exposed” and said “no other data appears to have been exposed.” The word “other” distinguishes the possible key exposure from other information; the notice still does not establish whether any key was copied or used.

2. A possible exposure is not proof of key use

The reported notice gives 00:30 UTC on October 17 as the earliest possible exposure time for users’ keys. That is a lower bound stated in a customer notification, not a confirmed access start, the first malicious request, the time of detection, or proof that a key was copied. The same reporting says OpenCode had not disclosed when unauthorized access began. The window cannot be reconstructed from the public timestamps alone.

Users did report charges. Colin Parsons wrote on October 17 that an OpenAI key had incurred $281 and an Anthropic key $168, both keys used only with OpenCode according to his post. The arithmetic is $281 + $168 = $449; Parsons described the spend as “>$400.” Those are two user-reported account amounts, not provider-audited charges or a verified incident total. BleepingComputer also relayed another user’s $1,700 report. Neither report establishes that the usage came from a stolen Console key or was part of model training.

Provider posts document response steps, with limits. Lydia Hallie wrote that Anthropic had revoked Claude API keys it believed were affected. Evan Otero said Google had blocked exposed keys and was working with affected customers and OpenCode. On October 18, Raad said OpenCode refunds were underway. On October 19, Tibo said OpenAI worked with OpenCode to restore access and refund API credits that would have been drawn in the attack. The posts document announced actions; they do not identify the operator behind the activity or independently verify each user’s bill or final reimbursement.

3. “Distillation” is the label; the bridge is unpublished

OpenCode’s public statement linked the breach to a “large-scale distillation attack.” It did not identify an actor or publish query logs, a model-output collection path, a training dataset, or other evidence explaining how exposed provider credentials were connected to model training. A key that can authorize paid requests could be used for many purposes. The public record does not establish which purpose applied here.

Researcher Nathan Lambert questioned the terminology, asking how theft of API keys related to model training and why the activity’s volume was being described as distillation. That comment does not disprove OpenCode’s characterization. It identifies the missing evidentiary step: public material joins a credential breach to a training-oriented label without showing the activity that connects them.

The disclosure was also explicitly provisional: OpenCode said the incident was ongoing and that it could not share more detail at that point. Through the October 24 research cutoff, the OpenCode disclosure thread and public GitHub security page reviewed here contained no technical incident postmortem describing the entry method, key handling, or evidence for the distillation claim.

Verdict

The strongest supported account is narrower than the headline label: OpenCode said a breach affected a defined share of Console users with linked providers; a reproduced notice warned that stored provider keys might have been exposed; customers reported charges; and provider representatives described revocation, blocking, or credit actions. Raad separately said the local app was not the affected surface.

The public record does not yet establish how the legacy system was entered, how many accounts or credentials were involved, whether the reported charges resulted from key exposure, or whether exposed-key activity was used for distillation. Those are material open questions, not proof of concealment or negligence. The next useful update would publish the exposure window and counting definition, explain the evidence connecting the activity to training, and distinguish confirmed credential use from suspected exposure.
