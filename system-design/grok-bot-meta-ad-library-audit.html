Grok's Meta Ad Library: What 91 Creatives Establish—and What 171 Doesn't

Sprites' September 21 Grok snapshot reports 171 detectable ads across Meta and Google: 91 on Meta and 80 on Google. Its analysis card separately reports 932 of 2,064 Meta ads still delivering. Sprites labels the 91-ad set “newest” and applies Grok Bot attribution only to the Meta creatives it says it read and identified. The 171 is a page total, not an established Grok Bot count, and the snapshot does not reconcile the 91 with 2,064.

TL;DR: The 91-ad Meta set is the one Sprites labels “newest”; Grok Bot attribution applies only to the Meta creatives Sprites says it read and identified. Sprites says 80 of the 91 newest ads launched “this week” in its September 21 snapshot. It reports 43 ads across 20 creator and publisher pages and 29 carrying #SpaceXAIpartner; the page does not say whether 29 is measured against 91 or 43. Sprites reports x.ai/bot as the destination for every Meta creative in its reviewed set for which a destination is provided. The 80-ad Google set is part of the 171, not additional inventory; six embedded preview objects carry image-production headlines but do not establish the product or destination of the full set. The creative counts describe inventory; the 932 is a reported delivery-status count, not performance.

Snapshot note: The live Sprites page has changed since the saved September 21 snapshot. On October 21 it reported 3,349 Meta ads and 1,526 running from an October 6 read. This audit uses the saved snapshot and does not combine the two reads.

1. Three scopes, one page

The saved snapshot opens with 171, described as “ads in public libraries,” split 91 Meta, 80 Google, and 0 LinkedIn. Its September 21 analysis card says: “932 of 2,064 Meta ads still delivering. 80 of the 91 newest launched this week.” The next card says: “20 creator pages post 43 of the 91 newest ads. 29 tag #SpaceXAIpartner.” The FAQ calls 171 an “observed library total, not a claim that every ad is running simultaneously.”

The 91 and 2,064 counts do not share a defined denominator. Sprites labels the 91-ad set “the 91 newest,” then reports 2,064 Meta ads without defining that population, date, or filters. It does not say whether 91 is a subset, a sample, or a separate filter. The 932 is explicitly a subset of 2,064: 932 / 2,064 is a 45.2% still-delivering share of that count. It says nothing about the share of the 91 or 171.

2. What the 91-ad set shows

Within the set, the product evidence is specific. Sprites says: “Every Meta ad read sells Grok Bot, landing on x.ai/bot. None sell the chat app.” The landing-page summary is a read-level claim, not a per-ad field for all 91. The saved page exposes only part of the set and gates the rest; this audit does not extend the summary to every ad. Only 95 of 171 ads were read and explained overall, and the page does not map that depth to the 91. The product claim is therefore a read-level finding, not a per-ad audit of all 91.

In its September 21 snapshot, Sprites says 80 of the 91 newest ads launched “this week.” It reports 43 ads across 20 creator and publisher pages and 29 tagged #SpaceXAIpartner; the page does not say whether 29 is measured against 91 or 43. The reported format mix is 75% video, 10% static, and 15% carousel. The longest observed run is 14 days.

Those counts describe creative metadata, not delivery. A 91-creative count is not 91 delivered ads; a library can show a creative that ran briefly to a small audience. The 14-day maximum is the longest observed run, not a test window. It could reflect a deliberate test, a late start, or a creative that stayed active; the library has no decision log.

A shared destination does not prove shared targeting. Sprites infers that segmentation, if any, happens after the click; that is a hypothesis, and the library publishes no targeting or post-click routing.

The 80-of-91 figure is descriptive: Sprites reports those ads as launched in the prior week. The page does not show how many were distinct experiments, how budget was allocated, which were stopped, or which performed. A launch push, routine rotation, and structured testing are all possible; the counts do not distinguish them.

3. Why the Google 80 cannot be called Grok Bot

The Google set is 80 ads: 64 text, 11 video, and 5 image, across eight markets. It also lists three vendor-reported impression-band entries: <1k, 7k–8k, and <1k. Three entries, two unique values, with no count per band and no mapping to particular ads, formats, or markets. Sprites says bands are measured only on Google and LinkedIn. They are delivery disclosures, not performance data.

The saved page embeds six Google preview objects, all with format “search” and four unique headlines, including “Image 2.0: Built for Real Work.” Three headlines are rendered. In those six, the body, call to action, destination, image, advertiser metadata, active status, and run dates are empty. The full 80 headlines sit behind a lock labelled “All 80 Google headlines.”

The available record does not establish that the Google ads sell Grok Bot, or that they sell something else. The “one product, one page” finding is explicitly Meta-only; the Google panel has no product label. The rendered headlines are image-production pitches, consistent with Grok’s image features, a separate image buy, or a mixed buy. The source does not say which.

A missing field in a preview does not prove the underlying record lacks it. The embedded previews are not a random sample, so they cannot estimate the full set; they also lack the fields needed to assign the Google set to Grok Bot.

4. Creators, hashtags, and the limits of distribution

Sprites reports 43 ads across 20 creator and publisher pages. The sampled creator copy is first-person testimonial. One ad says: “SpaceXAI just released Grok Bot, your team of always-on, delightful digital colleagues.” Another says: “Grok bot is the first piece of technology that has changed how I run my business, here's how it works.” The tag is a campaign marker. It signals a partnership or campaign affiliation, not the deal terms, the brief, or whether the creator initiated the post.

Meta's partnership ad format can feature the creator and advertiser in the ad header, and the Ad Library includes branded content. The library shows the pages and the tag. It does not show contracts, rates, selection logic, targeting, or organic endorsement. Twenty pages is twenty distribution surfaces, not twenty independent audiences. A feed sighting shows one account was served; the library shows the creative, not the auction behind it.

The platform frequency controls do not fill that gap. Google caps Display and Video; Display allows campaign-, ad-group-, or ad-level caps, Video only campaign-level; windows are daily, weekly, or monthly. Google's page does not list Search. Meta allows campaign-level controls with Advantage+ campaign budget or ad-set-level controls with Advantage+ off, not both. Neither page describes a cross-platform cap, and Sprites publishes no audience-overlap or frequency data. A sighting on Instagram and a Grok image ad on YouTube do not show a shared cap.

5. What the paid-media guide says

SpaceXAI's October 5 guide is the company's account of how its paid-media team uses Grok Bot, not an independent review. Miguel Cruz, who says “I run paid media for Grok Bot,” describes bots that pull demand research, draft budgets and channel structure, draft copy, brief creative, set up tracking, and report, with human approval. The guide says channel-specific bots take a first stab at copy and Cruz rewrites anything that looks off. It does not tie a specific Meta creative to Grok Bot authorship.

The guide names no ads or campaigns, publishes no spend, CAC, or result, and does not tie specific creatives to specific bots. It supports bot-assisted copy drafting in a workflow account, not final authorship of specific ads. A bot checks spend pacing and conversions each morning; the team hears about exceptions. That is monitoring, not an outcome.

Grok Bot opened in beta on August 11, 2026; the guide is dated October 5, two weeks after the snapshot. The dates fit a launch push, but they do not establish who wrote the ads.

6. The measurement gap

Meta's Ad Library lists active ads and creative metadata. Outside the EU/UK, active commercial ads do not show spend, impression ranges, total reach, or ad-level targeting, and stopped ads are not archived. Ads delivered to the EU/UK are archived for one year and show total reach and ad-level targeting, but not spend or impression ranges. Spend and impression ranges are reserved for social-issue, electoral, and political ads. Google's Ads Transparency Center is a searchable repository; targeting and recipient counts are available only in some regions. Neither publishes conversions or cost per result.

Sprites says cross-channel spend is not public and that spend is modeled. It lists CTR, CPC, CPM, conversions, Google keyword lists, and Meta audience targeting as unavailable, and reports no CAC or ROAS. Its Google impression bands are delivery disclosures with no count or per-ad, per-format, or per-market assignment, so they cannot be matched to the Meta count. The modeled spend figure is not used here.

What would change the verdict?

  • A denominator reconciliation from Sprites: define 171, 91, 2,064, 932, and 95, and state whether 91 is a subset, a sample, or a separate filter.
  • A per-ad product label and destination for the 80 Google ads, ideally from Google's records or a landing page that names the product.
  • A spend or reach disclosure for the Meta ads. That would turn an observed creative count into a distribution estimate.
  • A statement from Grok's maker tying specific creatives to Grok Bot, and a separate statement on who wrote them. The first would supply product attribution; the second, authorship.
  • A creator contract, rate card, or brief. That would turn the hashtag from a campaign marker into a distribution fact.

The snapshot supports creative volume and visible distribution. It does not establish delivery scale, performance, authorship, or personal targeting.

7. Three lessons from the evidence

Three lessons follow directly from the counts and fields above:

  1. The denominator belongs in the claim. “80 of the 91 newest” is supportable; “80 of the 2,064” is not.
  2. Treat “newest N” as a vendor label, not a verified sample. The 91-ad set can describe a push, but it cannot stand in for 2,064 without a stated filter and date.
  3. Audit per-ad fields before assigning a product. A page-level heading is not a product label or a destination; the six Google cards show why.

8. The verdict

What the snapshot documents. Sprites identifies a 91-ad Meta set it labels “newest,” 80 of which it says launched “this week” in its September 21 snapshot, alongside a separate 80-ad Google set. Within the Meta set it reports 43 ads across 20 creator and publisher pages, 29 partner tags, and x.ai/bot as the destination for reviewed creatives with a destination.

What remains unestablished. The 91 remains a vendor-labeled set, not a measured fraction of the 2,064 count. The Google 80's product and destination remain unresolved; the six previews are not a representative sample. The 171 is a page total, not a Grok Bot count.

What the evidence cannot show. The library reports creative metadata, not observed reach or spend, conversions, CAC, ROAS, or authorship.
