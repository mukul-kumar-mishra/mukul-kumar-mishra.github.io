Corrections and clarifications

Material corrections to published articles, with the old wording, the new wording, the reason and the source used.

Last updated October 25, 2026

This page records corrections that change a factual claim, a number, a date, a title, or the meaning of a published figure. Minor copy edits, formatting fixes and dead-link repairs are not listed. If you find an error, contact Buildopsy with the page and the source.

October 25, 2026 — Starlink core-network outage title

Old wording: “Starlink Core-Network Outage: How 8,000 Satellites Went Dark for 2.5 Hours.”

New wording: “Starlink’s 2.5-Hour Global Outage: The Core Network Failed, Not the Satellites.”

Reason: The article and its sources establish a ground core-network failure; they do not report a satellite failure. The old title implied the satellites went dark. The new title names the core network as the failed layer, not the satellites.

Source: Reuters, The Guardian and ThousandEyes; see the article’s Sources and Method.

October 25, 2026 — Starlink outage scale, figure and trigger wording

Old wording: "6M users across 140 countries dropped"; "8,000 satellites up"; "8,000 satellites shared the core's fate"; "healthy data plane"; "The trigger is unknown."

New wording: The service had 6M+ users across roughly 140 countries; tens of thousands were reported offline. The figure says "8,000+ launched," and fate-sharing now describes the service's dependency on one control plane, not satellite failure. ThousandEyes described a control-plane failure with a data plane that was sporadically functional but unreliable. The component-level trigger is not publicly documented; ThousandEyes relays a secondhand reseller report of an upgrade procedure.

Reason: The 6M figure is Starlink's subscriber footprint, not an affected-user count. "8,000 satellites up" is not supported; sources say more than 8,000 launched since 2020. The sources do not report satellite failure, so the glossary and figure were narrowed. The trigger wording now distinguishes a secondhand report from a SpaceX publication.

Source: Guardian, CNET, Straits Times, ThousandEyes and The Verge; see the article's Sources and Method.

October 25, 2026 — Starlink source attribution and glossary precision

Old wording: The July 24 section included a Navy drone-boat test; the report geography named Asia, Africa and Colombia; the 58,000-report row cited Reuters, Guardian and CNET; glossary rows said "all gateways," "everything downstream" and "millions of sessions"; the figure said "16% of normal traffic"; the queue card said "Recovery math."

New wording: The Navy drone-boat test is removed because the Reuters-bylined report dates it to August 2025, not July 24. The geography list is narrowed to North America, Europe, Australia and other served regions. The 58,000-report row is attributed to TelecomsTechNews and USA Today. Glossary rows now say gateways on multiple continents failed at once, downstream service failed globally with some links partially active, and the reattach surge is modeled. The figure says "16% of ordinary connectivity"; the caption ends at the public-record limit; the queue card labels recovery math as modeled. The section heading now says gateways across regions, and the social alt and SVG descriptions match the figure's blast, public-record-limit and ordinary-connectivity wording.

Reason: Each change brings the wording back to the fetched sources and removes claims the July 24 record does not support. The August 2025 USV test is a separate incident.

Source: ThousandEyes, The Verge, TelecomsTechNews, USA Today, and the Military Times/Reuters syndication for the August date; see the article's Sources and Method.

October 24, 2026 — GCP us-central1 status window

Old wording: The status-page header started at 07:44 and recorded a 4-hour-8-minute window.

New wording: The status-page header starts at 07:42 and records 4 hours 10 minutes. The detailed customer-impact window remains 07:41–11:52, or 4 hours 11 minutes.

Reason: Google’s incident page header says the incident began at 07:42 and ended at 11:52. The detailed addendum uses 07:41. The article now reports both without reconciling them.

Source: Google Cloud Service Health: us-central1 network incident.

October 24, 2026 — PHP2Shell warning window

Old wording: “roughly eighteen-hour public defender window.”

New wording: Three warning intervals: CVE identifiers roughly 22.5 to 23 hours before release, the Calif video roughly 18 hours before release, and Chainguard’s early images 6 hours 19 minutes before the 14:00 UTC release.

Reason: Different defenders first learned from different public artifacts. The article now reports the interval attached to each start time instead of treating them as one universal countdown.

Source: PHP Foundation and The Register.
